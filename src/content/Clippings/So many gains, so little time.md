## TL;DR\*

High-intensity, low-frequency training can offer remarkable strength and muscle gains while minimizing the training time investment necessary.

Maximal training intensity (and commensurate gains) can be ensured by stacking various intensifying techniques and going to "absolute failure".

Absolute failure entails sequentially hitting multiple failure points in a given movement.

Training with a few select compound and gymnastics movements offers "free-gains" — meaning strength that generalizes beyond the specific movement. There is no need to train a broad swath of exercises.

These movements are also readily adjustable (meaning intensity can be instantaneously manipulated) and allow one to more safely hit absolute failure.

Practical Implementation

Train 1-2 times per week.

Total training time will generally be 30-75 minutes per week.

Hit absolute failure with every movement pattern and then allow your body lots of time to recover.

1-3 sets per movement pattern.

Aim for low rep ranges to hit the first failure point and use "drop-sets" to hit higher rep ranges later in the same sets.

Make every rep as difficult as possible.

The five core movement patterns to train:

Hip Hinge (e.g., pistol squat)

Vertical Pull (e.g., one-arm chin up)

Vertical Push (e.g., handstand push up)

Horizontal Pull (e.g., front-lever rows)

Horizontal Push (e.g., planche push up)

## 1\. What Does HILF Strength Training Entail?

I'll note there are many HILF approaches; this is solely my interpretation and implementation as it applies to strength training.

Focus on intensity. Get to [absolute failure](https://app.notion.com/p/d6a8b7cd00e748dc948b4147cec3b6a7#b4c0868151584763ab8e953003d83d37) with each primary movement pattern.

Minimize training frequency: Strength train 1-2 sessions a week, totaling around 30-75 minutes of workout time per week.

Maximize recovery and time off. (The corollary to the previous tenet.) The training schedule allows lots of time-off between workouts to facilitate a full recovery and to help mitigate the potential for an injury.

Train a very limited set of exercises. Understand that strength and muscle gains carry over to other exercises that aren't directly trained, resulting in "free-gains."

Use primarily gymnastic rings-based bodyweight exercises for the upper body but with some supplementary weighted exercises for the lower body (e.g., barbell front-squat or weighted pistol squats.)

Enjoy all your extra free time. Go hiking, read a book, spend time in nature.

## 2\. The Theory Behind HILF Strength Training

Fundamentally, building strength and muscle is predicated on signaling a need for those capacities to your body. Your physiology itself is agnostic to how that is signaled - exhaustion is exhaustion regardless of your use of barbells, machines, or bodyweight exercises.

Conceptually, HILF training offers a very intense signal to your physiology as training to absolute failure is far more intense of a training stimulus than simply nearing or hitting failure points. It then allows a lot of time off for repair and recovery. High frequency, low intensity approaches offer more sedated signals, meaning more volume/training sessions are required. The gradual accumulation of those signals results in strength and muscle gain.

A HILF approach to training doesn't necessarily lead to more muscle or strength gain (although it may, particularly as the overall injury rate can be lower.) It is, however, preferable as the necessary time investment is attenuated, allowing more time for other interests and hobbies. Working out doesn't have to be a lifestyle - it can be merely an adjunct without necessarily reducing the desired strength or muscle outcomes.

Approaches that concurrently implement high frequency AND high intensity work if you make use of anabolic agents or other pharmaceuticals that can enhance recovery capacity. Otherwise, if used over longer time periods, you overtrain and risk burnout and injury.

You don't need to train every exercise or movement. Training a few core movements and building strength in those results in strength capacity that generalizes to other movements (i.e., "free-gains"). Gymnastic-derived and other compound movements better capture a wider range of strength capacity that generalizes more broadly. (E.g., training for one-arm chin ups necessarily improves how much weight you can bicep curl whereas training bicep curls has far less carryover to one-arm chin ups.)

### 4.2. Warm Ups

Generally, extensive warms up and mobility prep is unnecessary.

General Warm Up: Spend a couple of minutes to get your body active as a warm up. Think a few jumping jacks to get embodied and moving.

Movement Specific Warm Up: Do a partial regressed set of each movement prior to the first training set.

This set allows you to test out if everything is working and feeling as it should be, prior to maximal loading. For example, prior to training your one-arm chin ups, you might do a few reps of normal chin ups.

The exception would be with barbell front or back squats; here I think a more extensive warm up with several sets leading up to the training set is preferred.

💡

Having said all this, you know your body best. If you think you need to do a more extensive warm-up and joint prep and whatever else, do so. If you spend all day sitting or in an otherwise compromised posture, a more extensive warm up might also be advised.

### 4.3. Tools & Techniques for Maximizing Intensity

💡

One or more of these techniques should be "stacked" to make movements as difficult as possible. Some movements are more conducive to some techniques then others. (E.g., I would never apply negative-only reps to barbell squats due to safety concerns.) Also note that the delineations between some of these techniques are "fuzzy."

Accommodating resistance involves modifying a movement to make it more evenly exerting.

Typically, movements will have specific failure points. For example, in a back squat, lifters will almost always fail near the bottom of the movement. Applying accommodating resistance to a back squat would aim to make the middle and upper ranges of the movement more difficult. This could be accomplished by adding chains or [bands](https://www.bing.com/images/search?view=detailV2&ccid=xdSrkmCM&id=7C1A2555601640795B23C6907D51A1DD6FD2ED61&thid=OIP.xdSrkmCMYWnwo73UBYymJQHaGz&mediaurl=https%3A%2F%2Fwww.rubberbanditz.com%2Fmedia%2Fwysiwyg%2FSquat_ari_squat.jpg&exph=1928&expw=2100&q=Set+up+the+resistance+band&simid=608055047444693981&ck=8AED0F0E0511590B456F68B543072215&selectedindex=0&form=IRPRST&ajaxhist=0&vt=2&sim=15,16&ajaxhist=0) or having someone provide resistance or assistance at particular points in the movement. With other exercises like one arm chin ups, leverage points can also be manipulated.

Time-Under-Tension involves manipulation of the time duration of the reps/sets.

This is particularly important for the eccentric portion of the movement. Instead of taking 1-second down on a chin up, you might lower yourself over a 5-second count.

TUT is well-linked to hypertrophy so be sure to integrate this if gaining muscle is a primary motivation for your training.

Negative reps or eccentrics involve performing only the "negative"/"eccentric" or lowering portion of the repetition as there is greater strength in this part of the movement.

Drop sets involve failing on particular movement, then regressing it or reducing the load to prolong the maximal exertion.

For example, when training assisted one-arm chin-ups, you incrementally increase the support the other arm provides as you fatigue with the training arm. With weighted back squats, this might involve stripping weight from the bar to decrease load and then performing more repetitions.

Cluster reps/sets involve breaking up a set of reps with a break near the end to allow more reps to be completed in the "just-before-failure" threshold. (This technique can be analogized to "edging" during sex.)

For example, instead of doing 5 reps of bench press, you might do 4 reps and get near your failure point. You would wait 10 seconds, perform 2 more reps, wait 10 seconds more, perform 2 reps, and finally wait 10s and perform 1 more rep. This prolongs the period of maximal exertion in a set, as the last few reps are always the most difficult.

Rest-pause reps involve pausing the movement then resuming it.

Increased Resistance/Load involves increases the intensity of a movement by increasing the load.

For example, if you're performing a barbell back squat, an application of this would be increasing the weight on the bar or increasing the strength of the resistance band attached to the bar (set up as shown [here](https://www.bing.com/images/search?view=detailV2&ccid=xdSrkmCM&id=7C1A2555601640795B23C6907D51A1DD6FD2ED61&thid=OIP.xdSrkmCMYWnwo73UBYymJQHaGz&mediaurl=https%3A%2F%2Fwww.rubberbanditz.com%2Fmedia%2Fwysiwyg%2FSquat_ari_squat.jpg&exph=1928&expw=2100&q=Set+up+the+resistance+band&simid=608055047444693981&ck=8AED0F0E0511590B456F68B543072215&selectedindex=0&form=IRPRST&ajaxhist=0&vt=2&sim=15,16&ajaxhist=0). N.b., if you try this, please don't do the soul patch thing.)

Volume involves manipulating the sets and reps performed for a given exercise. Assuming all else is held constant, higher volume will result in greater signaling to a muscle.

For example, if you perform an extra rep of handstand push ups in each of your sets.

Volume is the intensifying technique of last resort; try to increase intensity through other means first.

I typically only aim to increase volume on training days I'm not capable of generating sufficient intensity through the other parameters.

#### The Most Intense Reps Possible

These "ultimate reps" generally involve:

a maximal tension isometric (a static hold),

hold a 3-5 second isometric at the start of the movement

an involuntary eccentric (the negative portion of the rep), and

increase the difficulty of the movement such that the eccentric happens involuntary but with some control. For example, slowing transitioning to a more difficult variation of the planche or having your partner provide more resistance.

a grinding concentric (the positive portion of the rep).

reduce the difficulty of the movement such that you are incrementally but barely able to move back up through the range of motion.

Those three steps are one full rep. Repeat that a few times for a set.

I don't believe this degree of intensity is always necessary but it can be helpful to implement sometimes.

You can get adequate signaling to your muscles and nervous system though slightly less (but still very intense) combinations of intensifiers. But, these "ultimate reps" are worth considering as the outcomes are excellent.

### 4.4. Training With Bodyweight Exercises vs. Conventional Exercises

While training with more traditional strength training exercises (e.g., barbell bench press) can be effective and can be conducted in a way that aligns with the principles laid out above, I would argue bodyweight exercises are generally preferable.

Training with bodyweight exercises allows you to more safely push yourself to absolute failure and more easily allows for the integration of accommodating resistance, as the leverage points and attendant difficulty of the exercise can easily be manipulated.

For example, if you're performing an assisted one arm chin up, you can at any point in the movement make the exercise slightly easier or harder by shifting the support arm. Contrast this to a [lat pull down](https://www.instagram.com/p/CIEJxtwHLMZ/?utm_source=ig_web_copy_link) where the weight has to be adjusted initially and is necessarily constant throughout the repetition. (Of course, if you have proper set up and spotters you can effectively opt for weighted exercises as opposed to bodyweight only as the spotters can dynamically alter the resistance throughout the exercise, as shown [here](https://www.instagram.com/p/By5qEavnkXa/?utm_source=ig_web_copy_link) with Martina pressing down on Philip on the way down.)

Equally here, I'd note there are generally more safety risks with weighted exercises - for example, with back or front squats, I never push myself to the same degree of failure that I can with unweighted/minimally weighted exercises. For a more detailed elaboration of the differences in risk/safety profile, see the FAQ [here](https://app.notion.com/p/d6a8b7cd00e748dc948b4147cec3b6a7#b4c0868151584763ab8e953003d83d37).

### 4.5. Measuring Progress

If you're applying (and particularly if you are stacking) these intensifying techniques, it becomes more difficult to assess progress. For example, with supported one-arm chin ups, it's very difficult to ascertain if strength is increasing because you're always tweaking the slew of variables to ensure the movement is maximally difficult.

One way to measure progress is to occasionally test other exercises with more normal parameters (although this wouldn't capture the full gains that are being made.) For example, to test your horizontal push progress by testing your bench press max every 3-months.

Another means of directly assessing progress is to occasionally film your first set of each movement. This can allow you to more objectively assess progress over time.

Muscular progress can be assessed far more directly, either aesthetically or objectively, with a tape measure/calipers or other tests.

💡

I'll note here I think it's a benefit to reduce outcome-attachment to the training at least in the short term. Systematize the training as much as possible and assess only once in a while. Longer-term trends will be evident, once you can progress a movement (e.g., moving from performing a tuck planche push-up to a straddle planche push up.)

### 4.6. Training Schedule & Implementation

Each week, train all the above mentioned movement patterns to absolute failure. Ensure this degree of exhaustion through compounding various intensifying techniques.

Personally, I train once a week. However, previously, I had split my workout out into two separate training days as it was easier to fit into my lunch breaks.

Try different splits and see what works for your energy and time constraints.

Perform 1-3 sets per movement pattern, depending on energy and time constraints.

Each movement should take 1-3 minutes per set, with all the drop-sets, negatives, and accommodating resistance integrated.

Alternate (or "superset") between two to three movements to allow for more rest. E.g., perform a set of tuck planche push ups (horizontal push) then rest 60s, then perform tuck front lever rows (horizonal pull). After 60s of rest, I would go back to the planche push ups.

### 4.7. Programming for Hypertrophy

After the initial release of this guide I received frequent questions around how to orient this HILF approach while still maximizing hypertrophy.

When approaching this question, I think it's important to flag that [HILF training may not be optimal for maximal hypertrophy (or maximal anything.)](https://app.notion.com/p/d6a8b7cd00e748dc948b4147cec3b6a7#66bc73800acd4256a0a6cba98fccaa37)

Having said that, I think it can offer quite good muscle building outcomes and we can further tweak the standard protocol to better ensure aesthetics.

Thus for hypertrophy aims, I'd advise:

Split the workout into two days per week (not one)

Emphasize time-under-tension on eccentrics and drop sets as intensifiers. (In addition to continuing to emphasize accommodating resistance.)

Aim for three sets per exercise, as opposed to one or two (not counting drop sets.)

Consider if you need to add a few further isolation exercises for lagging body parts (e.g., calves, arms). I'd generally advise training with the standard set of exercises for at least one month and seeing which muscles get insufficient stimulus.

These additional exercises should be trained at the end of each session, after the primary movements have been completed. They should be trained with the same parameters (e.g., applying various intensifiers), although fewer sets may be necessary.

Over the last year I've received extensive feedback from people around the world who've tried my HILF approach. I've also continued to iterate my training. Given everything I've learnt, I want to clearly articulate what I would view as the most reasonable implementation of HILF for your average Joe & Jane where the goal is sustainable and injury free long-term results.

Train twice a week (not once). 30-45 minute sessions should suffice assuming rest periods are narrow.

Limit the maximal intensity of the reps.

This might sound iconoclastic compared to what I've argued for elsewhere in this guide, but it's more about nuance. The objective is still to make workouts intense, but given risk/injury trade-offs, I would emphasize more incremental increases in intensity as opposed to always as hard as possible. Progress will be slower but more sustainable.

The [main exercises](https://app.notion.com/p/d6a8b7cd00e748dc948b4147cec3b6a7#61af71eecdbc41c587945256ea1e8dd7) should constitute 70-80% of your strength training.

Supplement these with a variety of ancillary exercises, e.g., RKC planks, Pallof Presses, Facepulls. These additional exercises do not need to be done beyond failure and should be trained after your primary exercises.

In terms of the relevant intensifiers, I'd emphasize time-under-tension. drop sets, and some accommodating resistance. Anecdotally, these seem the most sustainable.

Split squats are probably the best hip-hinge/squat movement to train. They are unilateral and build your ROM as you train them.

## 5\. How to Start?

💡

If you decide to try a HILF approach to strength training, follow the steps below to determine a workout starting point. I'd also advise committing to it for three months as that'll give you enough time to see the kinds of results you might hope for. If at that point it doesn't align with what you're looking for, feel free to return to whatever other pleb training methodology...

#### 5.1. Assess Where You Are At

First, figure out where you are with [each movement](https://app.notion.com/p/d6a8b7cd00e748dc948b4147cec3b6a7#f61179cae9274da580bc3f00e215e19e): that is to say, what variant of each movement can you perform at least three clean repetitions of? (And that is challenging enough that you can't do more than six repetitions? This is without adding any intensifiers.)

For example, with horizontal push, can you do pike push ups with your feet on the ground? If you can do six repetitions of this, try elevating your feet to the point you can't complete more than six clean reps. Once you've found the point where you can do between three and six reps, note this down. This will be your starting point for that movement.

The exploratory process needs to be repeated with all of the movements. Filming your movements is also incredibly helpful for documenting progress and keeping the assessments honest.

Enter all of this information into a table. Note the weight and elevation of the movement, if applicable.

#### 5.2. Intensify the Movements

Once this is complete, consider which [intensifying techniques](https://app.notion.com/p/d6a8b7cd00e748dc948b4147cec3b6a7#9034ef114dd64e219e88cd34b9739a47) can be added to each exercise.

Continuing with our horizontal push example, we might opt to emphasize time-under-tension and accommodating resistance, and to add drop-sets.

The time-under-tension aspect would mean you would ensure the eccentric/negative portion of each rep lasts at least 5 seconds.

The accommodating resistance would mean that you change the slant of your body as you're coming up to make it easier in the part of the movement that's harder.

The drop sets would mean as soon as you can't complete any more reps, you make the exercise easier (e.g., decreasing the elevation of your feet) and complete more reps.

## 6\. Summary

Stop training like a pleb.

High-intensity, low-frequency training can offer remarkable strength and muscle gains while minimizing the training time investment necessary. It efficiently signals a need for muscle and strength to your physiology.

Maximal training intensity (and commensurate gains) can be ensured by stacking various intensifying techniques and going to "absolute failure".

Absolute failure entails sequentially hitting multiple failure points in a given movement.

Integrate accommodating resistance before integrating any other intensifiers to exercises.

Training with a few select compound and gymnastics movements offers "free-gains" — meaning strength that generalizes beyond the specific movement. There is no need to train a broad swath of exercises.

These movements are also readily adjustable (meaning intensity can be instantaneously manipulated) and allow one to more safely hit multiple failure points.

Practical Implementation

Train 1-2 times per week.

Total training time will be 30-75 minutes per week.

Hit absolute failure with every movement pattern and then allow your body lots of time to recover.

1-3 sets per movement pattern.

Aim for low rep ranges to hit the first failure point and use "drop-sets" to hit higher rep ranges later in the same sets.

Make every rep as difficult as possible.

The five core movement patterns to train:

Hip Hinge (e.g., pistol squat)

Vertical Pull (e.g., one-arm chin up)

Vertical Push (e.g., handstand push up)

Horizontal Pull (e.g., front-lever rows)

Horizontal Push (e.g., planche push up)

If you aren't incredibly sore for at least a few days after your training session, that may indicate the intensity is insufficient. Remember, HILF doesn't work without the high intensity.
